What dose keeps this patient in the safe range?

Too little of a drug does nothing and too much is toxic, and patients clear drugs at very different speeds. Six blood samples after a first dose say how this patient handles it. Keanu learns that, with its uncertainty, and works out what a regular dose would do. The drug and the patient are fictional.

How Keanu answered this

The drug, its target range and the patient are all fictional, and the blood samples are synthetic. This is a demonstration of a statistical method, not medical advice, and must not be used to dose anyone.

The patient took one 300 mg dose and gave blood 1, 2, 4, 8, 12 and 24 hours later. Keanu fits the standard one-compartment model of a drug taken by mouth: it is absorbed from the gut at rate ka into a body volume V, and cleared at CL litres an hour. Patients differ, so what is known about the whole population (how clearance, volume and absorption vary between people) is the starting point, and the patient's own samples refine it. 

level(t) = D ka / (V (ka − ke)) × (exp(−ke t) − exp(−ka t))    ke = CL / V
CL ~ LogNormal(ln 5 L/h, 0.35)    V ~ LogNormal(ln 50 L, 0.25)    ka ~ LogNormal(ln 1.2 per hour, 0.4)
measured level ~ LogNormal(ln level(t), 0.15)

The No-U-Turn Sampler draws 1,000 plausible versions of the patient in your browser. Repeated doses add up, so once doses have built up the level over each interval is a sum of every past dose, which has an exact formula. For each draw Keanu computes that profile and reads off the time spent between 4 and 12 mg/L, the lowest level and the peak. Averaging over the draws gives the chances in the sentence. The suggested dose is the one, in steps of 25 mg, that keeps the level in range longest, preferring the lower risk when doses are close. Changing the dose or interval reuses the fitted draws, so it updates without refitting.

The assumptions are plain: the patient's parameters stay fixed over time, the drug is fully absorbed, each dose is taken on schedule, and measurement error is proportional, about 15%. "The population alone" repeats the decision with draws from the population instead of the patient's posterior, to show what the six samples are worth.

Keanu is a probabilistic programming library in Rust. Results are illustrations, not medical advice.
