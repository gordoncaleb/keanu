Will the hospitals run out of beds?

Five weeks into an outbreak, daily case reports are climbing. Keanu learns how fast the infection spreads from those counts, with its uncertainty, then runs every plausible outbreak forward to see how many hospital beds it will need, and what cutting contacts would change.

How Keanu answered this

The data are 35 days of reported cases in a town of 300,000 people. Keanu fits a susceptible, infected, recovered (SIR) model that steps forward one day at a time. Only some infections are reported, and daily counts scatter more than a Poisson count would, so the reports are negative binomial around a fraction of the day's new infections. 

new infections(t) = S(t−1) × (1 − exp(−(R₀ / D) × I(t−1) / N))
S(t) = S(t−1) − new infections(t)
I(t) = I(t−1) × (1 − 1 / D) + new infections(t)
reported(t) ~ NegativeBinomial(mean ρ × new infections(t), dispersion φ)

R₀ ~ LogNormal(ln 2, 0.5)     D ~ LogNormal(ln 7, 0.2)     ρ ~ Beta(12, 28)
I₀ ~ LogNormal(ln 10, 1)      1 / φ ~ Exponential(5)

The No-U-Turn Sampler draws 500 plausible outbreaks in your browser, taking gradients through all 35 days of the recursion. Early in an outbreak the counts mostly tell you how fast cases grow. Growth alone can't separate a high R₀ with a long infectious period from a lower R₀ with a short one, nor many infections with few reported from fewer with more reported, so the priors on the infectious period D and the reporting rate ρ do real work here. In a real outbreak they would come from clinical and survey studies.

For the forecast, each draw runs forward to day 200. The hospital side is deliberately simple and fixed: 2% of infections need a bed, seven days after infection, and patients stay eight days on average, leaving at a constant rate. Cutting contacts scales transmission down from the chosen day on. It reuses the 500 draws, so dragging it updates the forecast without refitting.

The case counts are synthetic, generated with a known R₀ of 1.8, an infectious period of six days and a quarter of infections reported, so you can see how well the model recovers them. The town, the hospital figures and the infection are invented; nothing here describes a real disease or health system.

Keanu is a probabilistic programming library in Rust. Results are illustrations, not medical or public health advice.
